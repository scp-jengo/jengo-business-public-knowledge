# Restoration Epistemology — Primary Sources Over Inherited Consensus

A methodology for reconstructing what was actually true rather than what has been repeatedly said to be true.

---

## The Core Problem

In many domains — historical, scientific, legal, ecological — knowledge is transmitted through secondary and tertiary sources. Each transmission introduces:
- Transcription errors
- Selection bias (what gets repeated)
- Interpretive drift (subtle reframing over time)
- Motivated distortion (claims that serve current interests)

After several transmission cycles, the circulating version may diverge significantly from the original reality. The divergence is often invisible — the secondary sources cite each other, creating an illusion of corroboration.

---

## The Restoration Approach

Instead of building on the existing consensus, start over from primary evidence:

1. **Identify what primary sources exist** — original documents, physical artifacts, contemporaneous records, first-hand accounts
2. **Reconstruct from those sources directly** — do not begin with the inherited narrative
3. **Document gaps explicitly** — where evidence is absent, say so; do not fill gaps with inherited assumptions
4. **Separate layers of evidence** — what can be physically verified, what requires inference, what is speculation
5. **Publish the methodology** — so others can check the reconstruction, not just the conclusion

**Key principle**: a gap remains a gap until evidence fills it. Inherited consensus is not evidence.

---

## Where This Methodology Appears

This approach is practiced across disciplines under different names, without a unified theoretical framework:

**Archival diplomatics** (Luciana Duranti): documents gain evidentiary weight through documented provenance and chain of custody — not through reputation or repetition.

**Archaeological stratigraphy** (Harris matrix): physical layers of evidence are read in documented sequence. The layer itself is the evidence; interpretations are labeled as such.

**Forensic methodology**: chain of custody establishes what can be claimed about physical evidence. Breaks in custody create documented uncertainty, not assumption.

**Historical "against the grain" reading** (Walter Benjamin): read historical records for what they suppress, not only what they assert. The marginalized detail often contains more truth than the central narrative.

## Practical Application

### When to use restoration methodology:

- When a domain has been dominated by a small number of canonical sources for a long time
- When contradicting the consensus carries significant social or professional cost
- When the stakes of error are high (financial, legal, historical)
- When physical evidence exists but has been subordinated to documentary or testimonial claims

### The four-layer assessment:

For any object of inquiry (document, artifact, ecological system, historical claim):

| Layer | Question | Method |
|-------|----------|--------|
| Identity | Is this what it claims to be? | Physical verification, primary registration |
| Integrity | Is it complete and unaltered? | Material analysis, gap documentation |
| Provenance | Where has it been? | Chain of custody reconstruction |
| Interpretation | What does it mean? | Explicitly labeled; separated from layers above |

Separating these four layers prevents interpretation from contaminating the physical record.

---

## The AI Training Data Implication

Restoration epistemology has a specific urgency in the current period: AI systems are trained on text corpora that heavily weight frequently-repeated claims. Minority perspectives, corrected errors, and primary-source findings that contradict established consensus are underrepresented.

**What is carefully documented today from primary sources becomes the answer AI gives tomorrow.**

Building primary-source knowledge bases is therefore not only historical or scientific work — it is infrastructure for future epistemic accuracy.

---

## Limits

Restoration epistemology is not the claim that all received knowledge is false. It is the claim that received knowledge requires verification, and that the method of verification matters.

Not every domain requires or permits full reconstruction from primary sources. Where primary sources are inaccessible, unavailable, or destroyed, restoration methodology documents the gap rather than filling it with assumption.
