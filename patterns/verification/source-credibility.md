# Source Credibility Assessment

A structured approach to assessing whether a source's output can be used. The question is not "is this source trustworthy" but "what does this claim require to be true."

---

## Core Principle

Credibility is claim-specific, not source-global. A source with a strong track record can publish a false claim. A source with a weak track record can be accidentally correct. Assessing source credibility without assessing the specific claim produces false confidence.

Treat source credibility as a prior, not a verdict. Strong prior: reduce verification effort slightly. Weak prior: increase verification effort. Zero prior: verify from primary sources regardless.

---

## Five-Step Check

**Step 1: Identify what the claim requires to be true**
Before evaluating the source, decompose the claim. What are the necessary conditions? A claim about statistics requires that the statistics exist, were collected correctly, and are being interpreted correctly. A claim about intent requires access to internal information. Map the requirements before assessing whether the source can meet them.

**Step 2: Assess whether the source has access to that evidence**
A media outlet reporting on classified information either has a legitimate source or is speculating. A researcher claiming clinical statistics either ran the study or is citing one. Ask: does this source have the type of access that would allow them to know this?

**Step 3: Check track record on similar claims**
Track record is specific, not general. A source that is reliable on financial data may be unreliable on political claims. A source reliable in normal conditions may fail under time pressure. Check track record for this type of claim in this type of condition.

**Step 4: Identify the interest structure**
Who benefits if this claim is believed? Does the source benefit? This does not automatically invalidate the claim — it identifies where additional scrutiny is required. Claims where the source benefits most from being believed are the claims to verify most carefully.

**Step 5: Check for correction culture**
Does the source correct its errors? How quickly? With what prominence? A source that never issues corrections is either never wrong (implausible) or does not disclose errors (significant credibility problem). A source that corrects prominently and quickly is demonstrating something real about its relationship to accuracy.

## Credibility Signals vs. Credibility Theater

**Real signals:**
- Documented methodology for how claims were obtained
- Named sources willing to be identified
- Track record of corrections
- Independence from the entities being reported on
- Willingness to publish findings that conflict with their stated position

**Credibility theater:**
- Professional appearance, polished design
- Institutional affiliation (affiliation is not independence)
- Large audience or following
- Awards from within the same industry
- Self-description as "fact-checking" or "independent"

The theater signals can be faked or purchased. The real signals require a demonstrated history and carry costs to fabricate.

---

## Track Record vs. Reputation

**Track record:** what a source has actually gotten right or wrong, documented with examples.

**Reputation:** what people say about a source, which reflects social positioning as much as accuracy.

Reputation can survive extended track record failure. Track record failures can be suppressed by reputation management. When they diverge, track record is the real signal.

---

## When Credibility Is Irrelevant

For claims that can be verified from primary sources, source credibility is largely irrelevant. Check the primary source. The question is then whether the source correctly identified and interpreted the primary source — which is a specific, checkable question.

For claims that cannot be verified from any available source (Tier 3 in the niet-achterlijk-protocol), source credibility is relevant but insufficient. Label the claim as unverified regardless of source credibility.
