# Claim Verification

How to verify a specific claim. The method is decomposition first, verification second — you cannot verify what you have not precisely defined.

---

## Core Principle

A claim that has not been disproven is not the same as a claim that is supported. "No evidence against X" and "evidence for X" are different epistemic states. The failure to find disconfirming evidence could mean the claim is true or that disconfirming evidence was not looked for. Be explicit about which situation you are in.

---

## Step 1: Decompose the Claim

Before attempting verification, break the claim into its constituent parts.

A claim like "this policy reduced crime" contains:
- A factual claim about crime rates (did they change?)
- A causal claim (did the policy cause the change?)
- A temporal claim (did the change follow the policy?)
- A comparative claim (was the change different from the counterfactual?)

Each part has different verification requirements and different difficulty. Identify which parts are verifiable, which require inference, and which cannot be verified at all given available evidence.

---

## Step 2: Identify Necessary Conditions

For the claim to be true, what else must be true? List the necessary conditions explicitly.

This surfaces hidden assumptions. A claim about an event requires that the event occurred. A claim about a person's motivation requires access to that person's internal state — which is never directly available. A claim about causation requires ruling out alternative causes.

Necessary conditions that cannot be met are not gaps — they are limits on what can be claimed.

---

## Step 3: Verify Parts, Not the Whole

Verify each decomposed part separately. A claim can be partially true — the factual component verified, the causal component unverified.

Label the verification status of each part. Do not aggregate into an overall "true" or "false" if the parts have different statuses. "The crime rate fell AND this policy caused it" may resolve to "the first part is verified, the second part is not."

---

## Step 4: Falsification Test

Ask: what evidence would show this claim is false?

If no evidence could falsify the claim, it is not empirically meaningful — it is a definition, a belief, or a frame, but not a verifiable claim.

If falsifying evidence exists and has not been found, note that the claim is unrefuted, not confirmed. If falsifying evidence has been looked for specifically and not found, that is stronger — note the scope of the search.

A claim that is structured to be unfalsifiable regardless of what evidence emerges is Tier 4 under the niet-achterlijk-protocol and should be treated accordingly.

---

## Step 5: Document the Verification Status

Every verified claim should be accompanied by:
- What was verified (the specific sub-claim)
- How it was verified (what source, what method)
- What was not verified (what remains at Tier 3)
- What would change the verification status (what new evidence would matter)

This documentation is not bureaucracy. It is the only way to revisit the verification as new evidence emerges.

---

## Common Errors

**Confirming the headline, not the claim:** a news article's headline is not the claim. Read the claim in context, including the methodology section if it is a study.

**Treating absence of contradiction as confirmation:** searching for counterevidence is different from being unable to disprove. Note which situation you are in.

**Causal arrow error:** verifying that A and B are correlated does not verify that A causes B. The causal direction requires its own verification.

**Sample-to-population error:** a claim verified in one context is not verified universally. Note the scope of the verification explicitly.
