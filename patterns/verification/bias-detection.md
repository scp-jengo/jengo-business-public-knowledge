# Bias Detection

A systematic approach to detecting bias in information. The goal is not to eliminate bias — that is not possible — but to map it so you know what distortion you are working with.

---

## Core Principle

Every information source has a perspective. The question is not whether bias exists but what kind, in which direction, and with what effect on the specific claim you are evaluating. A source can be biased and accurate. A source can be unbiased in presentation and misleading through selection.

Bias detection is a tool for calibrating confidence, not for dismissing sources.

---

## Types of Bias

**Selection bias:** which events or data are reported and which are not. A study that only reports statistically significant results skews the published record. A news outlet that covers only conflicts with one framing skews the picture of a conflict. The bias is in what is absent, not what is present.

*Detection:* ask what would need to exist in reality for only these data points to appear in this source's coverage. Is that realistic?

**Framing bias:** the same facts can be presented in ways that produce different impressions without any factual error. "Unemployment fell to 4.2%" and "nearly 1 in 23 workers remains unemployed" are both accurate. They produce different responses.

*Detection:* extract the factual claim from the framing. Evaluate the factual claim separately. Note the frame but do not let it contaminate the factual assessment.

**Source concentration:** when multiple reports cite the same original source, the apparent corroboration is false. The reports have not independently verified the claim — they have independently repeated it.

*Detection:* trace each report back to its original source. How many independent originating sources are there?

**Omission bias:** information that is technically accurate but missing context that would change its meaning. A statistic without a base rate. A trend without the starting point. A quote without the surrounding argument.

*Detection:* ask what context would change the meaning of this claim. Is that context present?

---

## The Base Rate Question

Before evaluating any claim about frequency, prevalence, or change, ask: what is the base rate?

"This event happened 5 times" is uninformative without knowing how often it usually happens. "Crime rose 20%" is uninformative without knowing the starting level and comparison period. "Source X was wrong about this" is uninformative without knowing how often Source X is right overall.

Missing base rates are one of the most common forms of misleading-while-accurate information.

---

## The Beneficiary Question

Ask: who benefits if this claim is believed?

This does not automatically make the claim false. It identifies where selection and framing pressure concentrates. Claims that benefit the source reporting them, or the funder behind the source, deserve closer examination of what might be missing.

The beneficiary question is a flag for increased scrutiny, not a verdict.

---

## The Absence Question

Ask: what would need to be true in reality, and what else would we expect to see if that were true? Is any of that absent?

If a company is systematically discriminating in hiring, we would expect to see disparate outcome data, employee complaints, and legal filings. If a source claims systematic discrimination based on one incident but none of these other indicators are present, the absence is relevant.

This is not proof that the claim is false. It is a calibration input.

---

## Mapping, Not Dismissal

When bias is detected:

1. Name the type of bias
2. Estimate the direction (which way does this bias push the conclusion?)
3. Estimate the magnitude (how much does this distort the overall picture?)
4. Adjust confidence accordingly — do not dismiss the source

A source with a known directional bias is still usable. You know how to read it. An unknown bias is more dangerous than a known one.
